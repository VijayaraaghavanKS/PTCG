# Title
Heuristic-Primary, Replay-Grounded Reranking for Pokémon TCG Agents

## Subtitle
A safe-by-construction hybrid that turned a ~735-rating heuristic ceiling into a 924.2 live peak — and the honest story of five rejected shortcuts along the way.

---

## 1. The problem, and why heuristics alone plateau (≈150 words)

Pokémon TCG is a game of hidden information (opponent hand/deck) and combinatorial choice (thousands of legal card interactions per turn), which the competition's own framing identifies as the core difficulty. A hand-tuned, rule-based heuristic is a natural starting point — it encodes real domain knowledge (energy sequencing, KO math, retreat safety) explicitly and predictably. But across every deck we built (three main archetypes, eleven abandoned ones), pure heuristic tuning converged to the same ceiling: roughly 690–735 live rating, independently matched by other public teams who reported the identical "~600–700 wall" on rule-based-plus-search designs. The wall wasn't a tuning problem — deeper search (4-ply minimax, up to 30 determinizations) and a genetic-algorithm weight search both landed at statistical parity with hand-tuned weights. Something structurally different was needed, not more of the same lever.

## 2. What we tried that failed, and why it's worth reporting (≈420 words)

The literature signal (public teams reaching 800–1034 on the same archetypes) pointed at behavior cloning (BC) on real ladder replay data. Our first instinct — train a classifier to imitate winning players' decisions directly, and let it *drive* — failed decisively, five independent times, across genuinely different attempts: more data (61 → 340 → 83 deck-specific games), added hand-engineered lethal/KO features, an ensemble-voting variant, and a turn-history-conditioned variant. Every one of them scored 2–26% locally against our own proven heuristic, regardless of offline classification accuracy climbing from 52.6% to 63.5%. Offline accuracy was actively misleading us.

The diagnosis, confirmed by re-reading the failure cases: a pointwise, per-decision classifier has no mechanism to represent a *plan* that spans multiple decisions in a turn — which attacker to save energy for, which damage counters to place toward a three-turn kill line. Our heuristic encodes that sequencing explicitly (a turn-plan object consulted by every sub-decision); a pointwise BC model re-derives each decision in isolation and breaks the plan's internal consistency even when any single decision "looks" locally reasonable. A parallel "plan-then-subordinate" turn-level architecture (a technique reported successfully by a public team) and a learned turn-value GBDT blend both landed at flat parity too — safe, but not a proven gain, suggesting the value is specifically in *constraining* BC to where sequencing risk is low, not in a bigger or better-conditioned BC model.

We treat this as a real result, not a wasted detour: it's the reason the eventual design is architecturally conservative rather than "BC bolted on top."

## 3. The design that worked: heuristic-primary, margin-gated BC reranking (≈430 words)

The heuristic always computes its full ranking of legal options first. A GBDT model trained on 340 real top-player ladder replay games (later extended to deck-specific retrains on 72–83 games) is consulted *only* when two conditions both hold: the heuristic's top options are within a small score margin of each other (a near-tie), and the decision type is on an explicit allowlist of contexts judged safe to override (single-pick, non-plan-critical decisions). Outside that narrow gate, or if the reranker errors in any way, the heuristic's own top pick plays, unmodified. This makes the design **safe by construction** — it cannot play worse than the underlying heuristic by architecture, not by hope.

Locally, this is nearly invisible: parity or a few points either way against the plain heuristic across every matchup we tested (49–64% band, thousands of games). Live, the same submission (ref `55464921`) debuted at 796.9, climbed to a peak of **924.2** — a 190+-point jump over the heuristic ceiling, and the single largest result of the project. This became the project's central methodological lesson: **local win-rate testing repeatedly proved unreliable as a predictor of live ladder performance, in both directions** (a striking example inverse to the failed pure-BC attempts, where good local signal predicted nothing). We treated this not as noise to explain away but as a real property of the evaluation (the μ₀=600 Gaussian-rating reset every fresh submission gets, confirmed by reading the competition's own evaluation documentation, means every submission's early score carries high uncertainty regardless of true strength) — and adjusted process accordingly: ship safe-by-construction designs, let live data be the actual judge, and stop over-indexing on local A/B deltas that don't clear real statistical noise.

We extended the same recipe to a second archetype (Mega Lucario ex), and pushed the training data further with **cross-play generation**: instead of one policy playing itself (tested earlier and rejected — it measurably hurt held-out accuracy, a "distillation ceiling" risk), we had many independently-evolved variants of our own agent play each other, keeping only winner-side decisions, mixed with real replay data as a substantial, non-diluted anchor. The result differed honestly between decks: on Mega Lucario ex, held-out accuracy improved monotonically (57.3% → 58.5% → 59.3%) as cross-play was added; on Dragapult, it was flat-to-marginally negative (63.0% vs. 63.5% real-only) despite 40,300 additional games — but it produced a real, twice-replicated +6.0-point live-architecture gain specifically against Mega Lucario ex, our own worst matchup (below).

## 4. Generalization, not just one good matchup (≈220 words)

The scoring criteria explicitly reward avoiding over-reliance on one matchup or situational advantage. Late in the project, we ran a deliberately broad verification pass — round-robin testing every current build against every other build we had ever made (including previously-rejected ones), then against a widened local reference set spanning archetypes we had never tested against before, then against every remaining untested opponent in our archive: roughly **22,000 local games** across three escalating passes. This surfaced real, actionable weaknesses that a narrower "does it beat our 3 usual opponents" test had missed — one candidate build (`dragapult_crossplay_reranker_v1`) lost to 8 of 16 sibling Dragapult variants and 2 of 6 Lucario-line builds despite passing every earlier, narrower A/B — and we reverted to its stronger predecessor as a direct result. We treat this multi-pass round-robin, not a single flattering matchup number, as the honest generalization evidence for the current live builds.

We also found and fixed twelve concrete bugs via game-log instrumentation rather than guesswork — including a Kaggle sandbox `__file__`-undefined crash invisible to local testing, a stadium card offered 2,377 times and played zero (an inverted play-condition sign), and a sequencing bug where a good Supporter card lost its one-per-turn slot to a worse card purely from decision order, not decision quality.

## 5. Deck construction and matchup targeting (≈350 words)

We piloted Dreepy/Drakloak/**Dragapult ex** (fast Ghost/Dragon evolution attacker, highest live ceiling and most complete evidence chain) as the primary deck, with **Mega Lucario ex** as a second, deliberately chosen line. That second choice wasn't arbitrary: mining our own 84 real ladder replays showed Mega Lucario ex was simultaneously our single most common real opponent (22.6% of the field) *and* our worst matchup project-wide (31.6% win rate) — a genuine, data-derived reason to prioritize it over, say, a matchup we simply found annoying anecdotally.

Two concrete, measured deck-composition decisions:
- **Card scarcity, not just card choice**: instrumentation showed 72% of Dragapult's missed Boss's Orders plays were pure hand-scarcity, not bad decision-making. We cut a genuinely dead card for our specific board (a 2nd Team Rocket's Watchtower — it only disables Colorless-type Abilities, and our researched wall matchup is Grass-type) for a 4th Boss's Orders (3→4, the legal maximum). Measured gain: the Mega Lucario ex matchup moved from a ~47–49% baseline to 56.5%, isolated and reproducible.
- **Dead cards are sometimes bugs, not design**: a card-text audit flagged Gravity Mountain (a stadium harmless to our own board) as a plausible cut. Instrumentation instead found it wasn't dead by design — a one-line polarity bug refused to ever play it into an *empty* stadium slot (offered 2,377 times across 150 games, played zero). Fixing the bug, not cutting the card, was the correct move; post-fix it plays roughly 1.5 times per game and produced a measured +3.2-point gain specifically against our one Stage-2-inclusive reference deck, exactly where the mechanic should matter — matchup-selective, not a blanket shift, which corroborates that the mechanism (not noise) is what moved.

Every deck change was tested in isolation, n=150–499 per matchup, before being combined with any other change — including finding and fixing a real negative *interaction* between two independently-good fixes before shipping them together.

## 6. Results and what's still open (≈180 words)

Current live-tracked pair: `dragapult_combined_final_v2` and `lucario_crossplay_reranker_v1`. The architecture's proven ceiling (924.2 peak, `bc_reranker_v2`) remains the project's best-ever live read, achieved through the safe-by-construction reranker design described above rather than through deck changes alone. We have not closed the gap to the leaderboard's top tier, and we say so plainly rather than rounding up: `lucario_crossplay_reranker_v1` still shows a real, unresolved local weakness against Alakazam-shaped decks (~35% win rate) that we chose to ship rather than delay further, and live-score volatility under the platform's Gaussian rating means any single day's number is a noisy read, not a verdict.

The transferable lesson we'd offer other teams: don't let local A/B testing be the last word on a design, especially for any technique whose value depends on tie-breaking or plan-level consistency — those are exactly the properties a small local sample is worst at measuring, and exactly where this project's biggest result and biggest false negatives both lived.
